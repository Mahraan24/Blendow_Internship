Teaching a Model to Recognise the Area of Law in Swedish Legal Documents

machine-learning
nlp
legal-ai
multilabel
How 8,625 Swedish legal documents, 18 main legal areas and 157 sub-areas taught me about labels, language, noise, and the difference between What a document is about versus Which legal area a document belongs to.
Author

Mahraan Talukder

Published

02 September, 2026

Context

Blendow (Blendow Group): Sweden’s leading legal information, media, and education provider. They are the umbrella organisation behind major Swedish legal platforms like Dagens Juridik and BG Institute. Read more

Lexnova (Blendow Lexnova): Blendow’s legal intelligence news agency and database. For years, it has delivered analysed court rulings, legal news, and expert commentaries, with a large library of Swedish legal documents manually categorised and tagged by legal professionals. Read more

Noxtua: A specialised European Legal AI platform, originally developed by tech startup Xayn together with leading law firms. It is designed for legal research, analysis, and document drafting and brings AI capabilities to legal workflows under European data protection standards. Read more

In short: Blendow-Noxtua is the joint launch combining Blendow’s Swedish legal data with Noxtua’s legal AI technology to serve Swedish lawyers and legal departments.

The Problem

For the big Blendow–Noxtua launch, we implemented a full AI document-processing pipeline to safely and reliably process Swedish legal content collected from various sources. The pipeline includes several steps, such as masking sensitive information, extracting metadata, converting documents to XML, and so on.

However, much of the content we collect has never been seen by the pipeline before. While documents already available in the Blendow Lexnova database had legal areas assigned by authors and lawyers over the years, new material collected from outside sources arrives completely unknown and untagged.

We needed a consistent legal-area tagging step so Noxtua could understand and process our data correctly.

In the AI era, the natural first instinct is: “Why not just use a Large Language Model (LLM) to sort and classify?”

The problem is scale.

We are dealing with thousands, if not eventually millions, of incoming documents. Moreover, running the entire pipeline is expensive on its own; adding an LLM just for classification would skyrocket both cost and processing time.

We therefore needed an approach that was faster, cheaper, and predictable enough to run as part of a larger processing pipeline.

That is where my research and implementation of a legal-area tagging classifier using NLP and sentence embeddings came into play. I used the labelled content already available in Blendow Lexnova to train the model, though there were quite a few challenges along the way.

  1. Multilabel Nature: Legal documents usually contain references, citations etc. for which a document can touch multiple legal areas to describe a case. Documents in Lexnova carry an average of 2.6 main tags.

  2. Imbalanced Hierarchy: The data features 157 subtags spread across 18 main legal areas. Offentlig rätt (Public Law) is the clear heavyweight with 26 subtags, while the other main areas on average has 7.7 subtags. This created a strong risk of Public Law dominating the rest.

  3. Mistreatment of Lexnova tags: A good number of documents on Lexnova were historically broadcasted across too many legal areas, as the main purpose has been to make a document reach as many of their subscribers as possible and not whether the document really belong to those tags. This increased false positives and reduced precision.

  4. Document Noise: Legal texts contain massive amounts of boilerplate noise such as headers, footers, procedural phrases, addresses, and contact details that do not really contribute to providing the legal area it belongs to.

  5. Complexity of Subtags: Mapping and maintaining all 157 subtags quickly proved complicated. For this reason, our primary focus shifted to mastering the classification on the 18 main tags first, while using a lighter approach for the subtags later on.

The Legal Area Tag Hierarchy

The labels were organised like a two-level family tree, with 18 broad legal areas at the top and 157 more specific sub-areas underneath them. A simplified part of the hierarchy looks like this:

Main legal area A few example sub-areas Total Sub-areas
Offentlig rätt (Public Law) Djurhållning (Animal Keeping/Welfare),
Kommunalrätt (Municipal Law/Local Government Law)		 26
Affärsjuridik (Commercial/Business Law) Associationsrätt-aktiebolagsrätt (Corporate Law-Limited Companies),
Sakrätt (property law or rights in rem)		 13
Miljörätt (Environmental Law) Buller (Noise Pollution),
Djur-och växtskydd (Flora and Fauna Protection)		 13

The complete hierarchy was much larger and uneven. Some branches contained far more sub-areas and documents than others. A single document could have several sub-area tags from different branches. This meant that the task could not simply be treated as choosing one legal area. The model needed to be able to assign several main legal areas to the same document.

For the experiment, I used the existing hierarchy to map each document’s sub-area tags to their broader parent areas. If several sub-areas belonged to the same parent, that parent was kept only once. If they belonged to different parents, all of those parents were kept.

For example, one document might have these sub-area tags:

[“Avtalsrätt”, “Fri rörlighet”, “Europakonventionen”]

Using the existing hierarchy:

  • Avtalsrätt (Contract Law) subtag belongs to Affärsjuridik (Commercial / Business Law) main tag.
  • Fri rörlighet (Free Movement) subtag belongs to Europeisk rätt main tag.
  • Europakonventionen (European Convention on Human Rights) subtag also belongs to Europeisk rätt (European Union Law) main tag.

The last two sub-areas therefore point to the same parent. The document therefore receives two main legal areas:

[“Affärsjuridik”, “Europeisk rätt”]

This step turned the original collection of 157 specific sub-areas into the 18 main legal-area labels that the model was trained to predict.

Creating the Label Matrix

The next step was to turn these labels into numbers using MultiLabelBinarizer. The result is a document-by-legal-area matrix:

  • 1 means the legal area applies to the document.
  • 0 means it does not.

For example:

Document Affärsjuridik Europeisk rätt Miljörätt Skatterätt
Document A 1 1 0 0
Document B 0 0 1 0
Document C 1 0 0 1

Document A therefore belongs to both Affärsjuridik and Europeisk rätt.

This matrix is essentially the model’s answer sheet. It does not contain the document text; it contains the legal areas that the model is expected to learn to predict.

Keeping the Labels Aligned

The documents were divided into three separate groups:

Training set: Documents the model is allowed to learn from.
Validation set: Documents used to evaluate the model while developing and tuning it.
Test set: Documents kept aside for the final evaluation.

The same separation must also be applied to the labels.

If a document goes into X_train, its labels must go into Y_train.
If a document goes into X_validation, its labels must go into Y_validation.
And if a document goes into X_test, its labels must go into Y_test.

In simple terms:

X = what the model sees
Y = what the model should predict

This ensures document features and their labels must remain aligned throughout the experiment.

Teaching the Model

During training, the model receives a document from X_train. It produces 18 scores, one for each main legal area. For example:

Example:

Affärsjuridik = 0.91, Skatterätt = 0.08, Processrätt = 0.72, Miljörätt = 0.14, …

These scores represent how strongly the model believes that each legal area applies to the document. The model can then compare its output with the actual labels in Y_train.

Suppose the correct answer is:

Affärsjuridik = 1 Skatterätt = 0 Processrätt = 1 Miljörätt = 0

A high score for Affärsjuridik is therefore useful, while a high score for Skatterätt would be a mistake. Likewise, giving Processrätt a very low score would be a mistake because that area is actually present.

These differences contribute to a value called the training loss. The loss can be thought of as a way of telling the model:

“How far were your predictions from the correct answers?”

The model then adjusts its internal parameters to reduce this loss. By repeating this process over many training documents, it gradually learns patterns that connect the document features to the legal areas.

In simple terms, the model is learning:

“When I see these kinds of words, character patterns, and semantic representations, these legal areas are more likely to apply.”

“What a document is about” Vs “Which legal area it belongs to.”

Before getting into the model itself, there is an important distinction that took me quite a while to understand.

In the early phase of the project I turned to topic modelling, specifically BERTopic, because it seemed like the most promising way to discover structure in the collection. If I could give the model thousands of Swedish legal documents and let it find natural groupings based on content, perhaps those groups would line up with the legal areas we already used in Lexnova. That would have been an elegant solution as most of the classifying heavy-work would have been easily done by just letting the data organise itself.

What actually happened was more revealing than I expected.

BERTopic did find coherent clusters. The topics were often tight, readable, and clearly about something specific. Looking at one of the runs, the model produced groups such as:

  • Court language about whether parties are bound by a valuation under AB 04 (a standard set of general conditions used for execution contracts in the Swedish construction industry)

  • Questions about which foreign taxes can be credited against Swedish tax

  • Consequence assessments under the GDPR when processing children’s personal data

  • Anti-money-laundering obligations that apply specifically to auditors and accounting firms

  • Hearing techniques and procedural strategies when children give evidence in court

  • Rules that apply when a company is sold and the purchase price has to be adjusted

  • Practical employment-law issues for managers, covering the whole journey from hiring to dismissal

Each of these topics is genuinely about a concrete subject. You can read the top words and the representative documents and immediately understand what the texts discuss. The problem is that “what the document is about” is not the same as “which area of law the document belongs to.” This became one of the most important lessons of the project.

A topic such as “hearing techniques when children give evidence” is a very specific procedural issue. In the Lexnova taxonomy it would sit under the much broader label Processrätt (Procedural Law). A cluster about money-laundering rules for auditors belongs somewhere inside Affärsjuridik (Commercial/Business Law) or a related regulatory area. A detailed discussion of foreign-tax credits is clearly tax law, yet the topic itself is narrower than the main area Skatterätt (Tax Law).

In other words, BERTopic was excellent at discovering the concrete subject matter of the documents. It was far less useful at recovering the more abstract legal-area labels we actually needed for the pipeline.

That is why I ultimately moved away from pure topic modelling as the final solution and towards a supervised multilabel classifier that was explicitly trained on the 18 main legal areas.

Document Text Clean-Up

Before trying to make a model understand the documents, I first had to make the documents understandable. This turned out to be a much bigger part of the project than I originally expected. Legal documents are not just paragraphs of useful legal information. They can contain XML and HTML markup, page numbers, addresses, telephone numbers, dates, case references, repeated sentences, OCR mistakes, names, and other pieces of information that may be useful to a human reader but can be distracting when trying to learn the broader legal area.

So instead of simply giving the raw document to the model, I built a cleaning step.

The cleaning process roughly works like this:

  1. Remove XML tags: As the pipeline will be sending XML text to the classifier, it is important to remove the XML tags and get one combined document text

  2. Strip noise: RegEx solutions were used to replace all the URLs, emails, phone numbers, page markers, personal numbers, organisation numbers, addresses and other boilerplate with an empty string (““)

  3. Normalise dates and legal references (SFS, case numbers, etc.) into simple placeholders: RegEx solutions were used to simply replace legal references like Swedish Code of Statutes (SFS) and case numbers etc with placeholder words like “SFS”, “CASENUMBER” etc.

  4. Collapse extra whitespace and dividers: to remove whitespaces and get a chunk of text for easier work or sentence persing.

  5. Optionally remove exact or near-duplicate sentences: legal documents could often have duplicate repeated sentences,

  6. Run spaCy: tokenise, optionally drop person names, keep surface forms and lemmas: spaCy was used to replace actual people’s names with a placeholder word PERSON using the ner solution. Further more the entire text is lemmatised, meaning that all the words in the documents turns to there root form of that word.

  7. Produce two outputs: clean_text (original words) and lemma_text (lemmatised version).

Lemmatization is particularly useful for Swedish because words can change their form and Swedish compound words can become very long.

The point is not to remove as much text as possible but to remove information that describes the document itself rather than the legal subject of the document. That distinction became surprisingly important.

Three different ways to read a Document

A document can tell us something through the individual words it uses, through the smaller pieces inside those words, and through the meaning of whole sentences. So I gave the model three different views of the same document.

1. Looking at the Important Words

The first view is the most traditional one.

The model looks at which words appear in a document and how important they are compared with the rest of the collection. This is TF-IDF.

A simple way of thinking about it is:

If a word appears everywhere, it probably tells us very little. If a word appears frequently in documents about one particular legal area, it may be much more useful.

For example, generic legal words like Lag (Law), Dom (Judgment) or Mål (Case) appear in almost every document and therefore is not very helpful for deciding the area of law.

On the other hand, a more specialised term like Arbetare (Worker/Employee) repeatedly appears in employment-law and the word Patent in intellectual-property-law documents. Since these words are specific to a particular legal area, they can be much more useful.

I used TF-IDF on the lemmatised Swedish text, lemmatisation gets all the words to their root form, so words like ska, skall and skulle all turns to their root form skola (shall / will), has shown in the table below:

Swedish word Lemma English example

2. Looking Inside Swedish Words

The second view was at the character level.

This may sound strange at first. Instead of only asking “what words are here?”, the model also looks for “what small patterns inside the words are present?”

This is especially useful for Swedish as a language, as Swedish can create long compound words, where several concepts are joined together into one word. Two words can therefore be related even when the exact full word has not appeared before.

3. Looking at the Meaning of the Text

The third view is quite different.

Instead of simply counting words, I used a Swedish sentence-embedding model called KBLab/sentence-bert-swedish-cased. This model has a maximum input length of 384 tokens. Therefore, before creating an embedding, I limited each document to its first 384 tokens. This first 384 tokens always includes the title and introduction of a document, which are often the most valuable components for predicting the legal area of a document. Any text after those 384 tokens was not used for the embedding.

This was important as passing the full documents into the embedding model would take considerably longer processing time, even though the model could only process up to its maximum input length. Explicitly limiting the input made the behaviour predictable by avoiding time preparing text that would not contribute to the final embedding.

The embedding model then converts those 384 tokens into a 768-dimensional numerical representation, often called an embedding. The idea behind an embedding is easier to understand if we imagine turning each document into a point on a map. Documents with similar meanings should end up relatively close together, while documents with very different meanings should end up further apart.

So instead of only learning that two documents contain similar words, the classifier receives another signal about whether the documents are semantically similar.

This is useful because two legal documents can discuss similar ideas without using exactly the same vocabulary.

Combining the dimensions together

At this point I had three different ways of looking at the same document:

Words which answers “What important terms does it use?”

Character patterns answers “What Swedish word structures and fragments does it contain?”

Meaning answers “What is the document broadly talking about?”

None of these views is perfect on its own.

But together they provide a much richer description of the document.

The final model therefore combines the three representations into one feature vector for each document.

The resulting feature vector is then passed to the classifier.

The Classifier

The final classifier is a small neural network called a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). The important thing is not really the name but the concept. It works like a series of decision-making layers.

The first layer receives all the information we extracted from the document in the 2000+ feature vectors, shown above. The following layers gradually combine these signals. At the end, the model produces 18 separate answers, one for each main legal area. This is important because a document does not necessarily belong to only one area.

So the model does not simply answer:

“This document belongs to ‘X’ legal area.”

Instead, it answers:

“How likely is this document to belong to each of these 18 legal areas?”

The system can then select more than one area when appropriate.

Model Scores and Evaluation

The figure above shows the model’s output scores for the different legal areas. Each document receives one score for each of the 18 labels. These scores represent how strongly the trained classifier associates the document with each legal area. The important point is that these scores are not simply one final class. Several areas can receive high scores for the same document.

For example, a document might receive something conceptually like:

Affärsjuridik: 0.91 Försäkringsrätt: 0.78 Skatterätt: 0.12 Processrätt: 0.08

The system can therefore select both Affärsjuridik and Försäkringsrätt rather than forcing the document into a single category.

To turn these scores into labels, a decision threshold is applied. Scores above the chosen threshold are treated as positive predictions, while scores below it are treated as negative predictions.

A model that uses a very low threshold will tend to assign more legal areas. That can increase recall, because fewer relevant labels are missed, but it can also reduce precision, because more incorrect labels are added.

A higher threshold does the opposite: it produces fewer predictions, potentially improving precision but missing some genuinely relevant areas. This is especially important in this dataset because the historical Lexnova labels are themselves not perfectly clean.

Confusion Matrix

The confusion matrix gives another way to inspect the model’s behaviour.

For a multilabel problem, the matrix can be interpreted label by label: for each legal area, we can examine how often the model correctly identified that area, missed it, or incorrectly assigned it.

The main quantities are:

  • True Positive (TP): The document belongs to the legal area and the model predicted it.
  • False Positive (FP): The model predicted the legal area, but the document was not labelled with it.
  • False Negative (FN): The document was labelled with the legal area, but the model failed to predict it.
  • True Negative (TN): The document does not belong to the legal area and the model correctly left it unassigned.

From these values we can calculate standard classification metrics:

Precision asks When the model predicts a legal area, how often is it correct?
Recall asks Of the documents that should have received a legal area, how many did the model find?
F1 score combines precision and recall into a single measure, balancing the two.

For this project, looking at these metrics per legal area is more informative than relying on one overall accuracy number. A model could achieve a seemingly strong overall result while performing poorly on smaller or less frequent legal areas.

The 157 Sub-Areas Problem

The 18 main areas were only the beginning. Behind them were 157 more specific sub-areas. At first, I wanted to solve the whole hierarchy at once. But this quickly became messy. There are many possible relationships between a main area and its sub-areas, and some documents can legitimately touch several parts of the hierarchy. Trying to solve all of this before properly understanding the 18 main areas would have made it much harder to understand where mistakes were coming from.

So I deliberately narrowed the first version of the problem.

  1. First: Can I reliably recognise the broad legal area?

  2. Then: Can I make the classification more specific?

This also makes the system easier to maintain. The current production classifier is therefore focused on the 18 main areas, while the same general architecture can later be extended towards the finer-grained labels.

Making It Something I Could Actually Use

A model that only exists inside a notebook is not particularly useful.

I wanted to be able to paste a real Swedish legal document into the system and see what it thinks.

So I built a small Streamlit interface around the classifier.

The interface allows me to:

  1. paste a document;
  2. run the classifier;
  3. see the predicted legal areas;
  4. see the model’s scores for the different areas;
  5. correct the prediction by manual input;
  6. save the corrected result in a CSV file.

This last part is particularly important. The interface is not only a demonstration. It also gives me a way to challenge the model. If I see that the model repeatedly assigns the wrong legal area to a certain type of document, that gives me something concrete to investigate. The saved results can then be used to compare the predicted labels against the corrected labels (that I manually enter) and calculate things such as precision, recall and F1 score. The application also checks whether a document title was already present in the training data from the JSON file of all the documents’ titles saved as hashes. Before pasting a document I first paste the title to check whether it matches with any of the hashed train document title. This is useful because a prediction on something the model has effectively seen before can give a misleadingly optimistic impression of how well the model performs.

From Notebook to Production

The final step was making sure that the model did not depend on the original training notebook. Once the model has been trained, I do not need to keep all 8,625 documents sitting beside it just to classify one new document. Instead, the important parts of the trained pipeline are saved into a single joblib artefact. This contains the information needed to process a new document in the same way as the training documents, including the vocabulary selection, feature configuration, MLP model weights, etc.

When a new document arrives, the production wrapper can therefore do roughly this:

New document

  1. clean it

  2. create the different representations

  3. combine them

  4. pass them through the trained model

  5. return the predicted legal areas

This was an important transition for me because it changed the project from:

“I trained a model in a notebook.” into “I have a small component that can actually be plugged into another system.”

What I Learned From the Project

The biggest lessons were not really about neural networks.

They were about the data.

Labels Are Not Always Ground Truth

Just because a document has been labelled by a human does not automatically mean the label is perfect. In this case, the existing labels were created for an editorial purpose, not specifically for training a machine-learning classifier. That means a label can be useful for a human reader while still being noisy from a machine-learning perspective. The model therefore learns from the labels we give it, including their mistakes.

More Information Is Not Always Better

My first instinct was to give the model as much information as possible. But legal documents contain an enormous amount of information that has nothing to do with their broad legal area. More text can therefore mean more noise. Cleaning and selecting the right information became just as important as choosing the model.

Where I Am Now

The current system can take an unseen Swedish legal document and produce one or more predictions across the 18 main legal areas. Additionally, The Streamlit app around the model improves the quality of the information going into it and the labels it learns from. That means looking more closely at difficult documents, understanding where the existing Lexnova labels are too broad, building a reliable manually reviewed validation set, and eventually extending the system towards the 157 sub-areas.
