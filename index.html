Teaching a Model to Recognise the Area of Law in Swedish Legal Documents

How 8,625 Swedish legal documents, 18 main legal areas and 157 sub-areas taught me about labels, language, noise, and the difference between What a document is about versus Which legal area a document belongs to.

Context

Blendow (Blendow Group) is Sweden’s leading legal information, media and education provider. Behind platforms like Dagens Juridik and BG Institute. Read more

Lexnova (Blendow Lexnova) is their legal intelligence database. For years it has delivered analysed court rulings, news and expert commentaries, all manually tagged by legal professionals. Read more

Noxtua is a European Legal AI platform focused on research, analysis and document drafting under strict European data rules. Read more

In short: Blendow-Noxtua combines Blendow’s Swedish legal data with Noxtua’s AI technology for Swedish lawyers and legal departments.

The Problem

For the Blendow–Noxtua launch we built a full AI pipeline to process Swedish legal documents. The pipeline masks sensitive information, extracts metadata, converts files to XML, and more.

The problem was new material. Documents already in Lexnova had legal areas assigned by lawyers. New documents arriving from outside sources had none.

We needed a reliable way to tag legal areas so Noxtua could work with the data.

The obvious idea was to use a large language model. But the scale made that impractical. We were dealing with thousands of documents, and eventually millions. Running an LLM on top of an already expensive pipeline would have been too slow and too costly.

So I built a lighter, faster classifier using the labelled Lexnova data that already existed. There were several challenges:

  1. Documents often belong to more than one area. On average a Lexnova document carries 2.6 main tags.
  2. The hierarchy is uneven. There are 18 main legal areas and 157 sub-areas. Public Law alone has 26 sub-areas; most others have far fewer.
  3. The existing tags were not perfect. Many documents had been tagged broadly so they would reach more subscribers, not because they truly belonged to every tag.
  4. Legal documents are noisy. Headers, footers, addresses, case numbers and repeated procedural language add little signal about the actual legal area.
  5. 157 sub-areas were too complex at the start. I decided to master the 18 main areas first.

The Legal Area Hierarchy

The labels form a two-level tree: 18 broad main areas and 157 more specific sub-areas underneath them.

A single document can touch several branches at once. So the model had to be able to assign more than one main area to the same document.

I mapped every sub-area tag up to its parent main area. If several sub-tags pointed to the same parent, that parent was kept only once. If they pointed to different parents, all of those parents were kept.

Example:

A document tagged with ["Avtalsrätt", "Fri rörlighet", "Europakonventionen"]

becomes

["Affärsjuridik", "Europeisk rätt"]

This turned the original 157 sub-areas into the 18 main labels the model would learn to predict.

“What a document is about” vs “Which legal area it belongs to”

Early on I tried topic modelling with BERTopic. The idea was simple: let the documents organise themselves into natural groups and hope those groups matched the legal areas we already used.

BERTopic did find coherent topics. It discovered clusters about things like:

  • whether parties are bound by a valuation under AB 04
  • which foreign taxes can be credited against Swedish tax
  • GDPR assessments when processing children’s data
  • anti-money-laundering rules for auditors
  • hearing techniques when children give evidence
  • price adjustments when a company is sold
  • practical employment-law issues from hiring to dismissal

These topics were clear and useful. The problem was that “what the document is about” is not the same as “which area of law it belongs to.”

A detailed discussion of hearing techniques for children sits under the broad label Procedural Law. A cluster about money-laundering rules for auditors belongs somewhere inside Commercial Law. A narrow tax-credit question is still Tax Law, even if the topic itself is more specific.

BERTopic was excellent at finding concrete subject matter. It was much less useful at recovering the abstract legal-area labels the pipeline needed. That became one of the most important lessons of the project.

I therefore moved to a supervised multilabel classifier trained directly on the 18 main legal areas.

Cleaning the Text

Before the model could learn anything useful, the documents had to be cleaned. Legal texts contain a lot of material that describes the document itself rather than its legal subject: XML tags, page numbers, addresses, phone numbers, case references, repeated sentences and personal names.

The cleaning steps were roughly:

  • remove XML tags
  • strip URLs, emails, phone numbers, addresses and other boilerplate
  • replace dates and legal references with simple placeholders
  • collapse extra whitespace
  • optionally remove near-duplicate sentences
  • replace person names with a placeholder and apply lemmatization

Lemmatization means turning every word into its base form. For example, the Swedish words ska, skall and skulle all become skola. This is especially useful for Swedish, where words change form a lot and compound words can be very long.

The goal was not to remove as much text as possible. It was to remove information that was irrelevant to the legal area.

Three Ways of Looking at a Document

I gave the model three different views of the same text.

1. Important words (TF-IDF) TF-IDF is a classic way of measuring how important a word is in a document compared with how common it is across all documents. Generic legal words like “lag”, “dom” or “mål” appear everywhere and help little. More specialised terms such as “arbetare” or “patent” carry stronger signals about the legal area. I ran TF-IDF on the lemmatised Swedish text so different forms of the same word were treated as one.

2. Patterns inside Swedish words Swedish creates long compound words. Looking at short character sequences (groups of 3–5 characters) lets the model recognise related terms even when the exact full word has never been seen before.

3. Overall meaning (sentence embeddings) I used a Swedish sentence-embedding model from KBLab (KBLab/sentence-bert-swedish-cased). An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of numbers that capture its meaning. Documents that discuss similar ideas end up close to each other in this number space, even if they use different vocabulary. I limited each document to its first 384 tokens (roughly the title and introduction), because those parts usually contain the strongest clues about the legal area.

None of these views is perfect on its own. Together they give a much richer picture of the document. The three representations are combined and passed to the classifier.

The Classifier and How Well It Works

The classifier is a small neural network called a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). It works like a series of decision layers. It receives the combined features from the three views above and outputs 18 scores — one for each main legal area.

It does not force a document into a single category. It answers:

How likely is this document to belong to each of these 18 areas?

A decision threshold then turns the scores into final labels. Looking at results per legal area was more informative than a single overall accuracy number.

Focusing on the 18 Main Areas

Behind the 18 main areas sit 157 sub-areas. At first I wanted to solve the whole hierarchy at once. That quickly became messy. There are many possible relationships between main areas and sub-areas, and documents can legitimately touch several parts of the tree.

I deliberately narrowed the problem:

  1. First, can I reliably recognise the broad legal area?
  2. Later, can I make the classification more specific?

The production system therefore focuses on the 18 main areas. The same architecture can later be extended to the finer labels.

Making It Usable

A model that only lives inside a notebook is not very useful. I built a small Streamlit interface so I could paste a real Swedish legal document and see what the model thinks.

The interface lets me:

  • paste a document
  • run the classifier
  • see the predicted areas and their scores
  • correct the prediction manually
  • save the corrected result

This is not only a demo. It is also a way to challenge the model and collect better examples. The saved corrections help measure real performance and improve the system over time.

From Notebook to Production

Once the model was trained, the important parts of the pipeline (the vocabulary, the feature settings, and the model weights) were saved into a single artefact. A new document can now be cleaned, turned into the same three representations, passed through the model, and returned with predicted legal areas — without needing the original 8,625 training documents.

That changed the project from “I trained a model in a notebook” into “I have a component that can actually be plugged into another system.”

What I Learned

The biggest lessons were about the data, not the neural network.

Labels are not always ground truth. The existing tags were created for editorial purposes, not for training a machine-learning model. A label that is useful for a human reader can still be noisy for a classifier. The model learns from the labels we give it, including their imperfections.

More information is not always better. My first instinct was to give the model as much text as possible. Legal documents contain a great deal of material that has nothing to do with their broad legal area. Cleaning and selecting the right information became just as important as choosing the model itself.

Where I Am Now

The current system can take an unseen Swedish legal document and produce one or more predictions across the 18 main legal areas. The Streamlit app lets me inspect difficult cases, correct mistakes, and gradually improve the quality of the labels the model learns from.

Next steps include building a more reliable manually reviewed validation set and, later, extending the system towards the 157 sub-areas.
